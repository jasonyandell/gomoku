The project

9×9 free-style gomoku, AlphaZero-style, on one laptop

The model learns gomoku from self-play: PyTorch on Apple MPS, the whole pipeline on a single M5 Max. The network is "small" (≈316k params); the replay buffer holds 1.5M positions. Because the board is a square, every recorded position is multiplied through the D4 symmetry group (4 rotations × 2 reflections = ×8 examples) before it reaches SGD — which is why the throughput metric we tune against is aug/s, augmented positions per second.

The central question is not "can it run faster?" It is: what training loop reliably teaches it to defend — not just to imitate the fast attacks its own search keeps finding? The recurring failure mode is fast-attack collapse: policy targets sharpen around attacks, self-play opponents fail to punish missing defense, and fixed heuristic / lookahead opponents then expose the gap.

Piece 1

The training machine

One loop: the model plus MCTS generate games, positions land in the replay buffer, SGD fits the network, and the fresh weights are pushed back to the players. Repeat. That's the entire AlphaZero machine.

The loop. Generate → buffer → SGD → push weights → repeat. Every game yields plies × 8 training examples via D4 augmentation; the buffer caps at 1.5M positions (the practical MPS ceiling — 17 planes × 81 cells stays just under MPSGraph's INT_MAX).

Production topology: one trainer, eight workers, one eval — all on one MPS device

In production (run_sweep.py --cell <CELL>) the loop is split across processes that all share the single GPU: 1 trainer + 8 self-play workers + 1 eval_worker, run in wave-mode — a per-version tile barrier keeps every worker on the same model version. Eval is inside the bundle: the eval_worker writes eval/model_elo to eval_results.jsonl. There is no separate eval step to schedule.

Production bundle. Verified in run_sweep.py: 1 trainer + 8 selfplay_workers + 1 eval_worker, wave-mode. All ten processes contend for the same GPU — which is exactly why the lab schedules them serially (see Piece 3).
A SIGTERM is a clean stop: the trainer finishes the current epoch, force-saves a fully-resumable latest.pt (replay buffer embedded), then exits 0. A later --resume latest.pt continues with no cold buffer refill. That property is what makes the next piece possible.
Piece 2

The research lab — and the two queues

The lab is a receipt-driven scientific loop, not a benchmark runner. Its scheduler splits work by hardware requirement, not by work type. There are exactly two queues.

Two queues, split by hardware. The left lane is serial because everything in it wants the one GPU. The right lane is parallel because nothing in it does — code and wiki work fan out as Agent subagents in worktrees and integrate as merge commits. The orchestrator never blocks code behind a GPU cell.

Receipts + Reviewer, in one line: every measurement files a promote | reject | needs_repeat | blocked receipt across five surfaces — and no promotion lands without a fresh Reviewer agent returning APPROVE (it reads files, never edits; verdicts are APPROVE / REVISE / BLOCK). The receipt is the lane.

Piece 3

Time-capped slices on the serial lane

The seam that ties the machine to the lab: a training run that knows how to stop on a wall-clock budget and resume cleanly. That turns an open-ended run into a schedulable slice — the same kind of item as a perf cell or an eval.

python scripts/run_sweep.py --cell <CELL> \
    --resume sweep_runs/<CELL>/checkpoints/latest.pt \
    --max-wall-secs 600 --final-eval

At the next epoch boundary past the cap, the trainer self-caps: force-saves a fully-resumable latest.pt (buffer embedded), tears the workers + eval down cleanly, and exits 0. --final-eval then runs one eval_worker cycle so the slice ends on a fresh eval/model_elo line. The lab reads Δelo from there. Resume never cold-refills the buffer.

The 10-minute-slice idea. A training slice, an eval probe, a perf cell, and a resumed slice all take their turn on one lane. Each slice is self-contained, resumable, and ends with its own eval — so a long training run interleaves with other GPU work instead of monopolizing the machine.

Why the lane is serial — measured, not chosen

We ran two full production runs at once and decomposed the trainer logs into engine-invariant rates. The verdict: each run drops to roughly half speed. But the two sides behave oppositely when you sum them:

1 run (solo)2 runs (sum)aggregate Δ
generation (moves/sec)392.7425.1+8%
SGD (steps/sec)26.624.2−9%

Self-play has MPS-occupancy slack (low, gappy load → interleavable → aggregate generation actually rises 8%). SGD has none: two trainers run tight back-to-back forward+backward loops at batch=512, collide directly, and lose ~9% of total work to the contention. So one serial tenant at a time is correct. The parallelism lives in the everything-else queue.

Composition

How the pieces compose

Nothing here is clever in isolation. The leverage is in the stacking: the machine plugs into the serial queue as a slice; the lab orchestrates both queues; campaigns sit on top and just ask for GPU time.

The whole stack. A training run is just a GPU-required item — same line as a perf cell or an eval. Exploration, in the lab's words, is simply get in the GPU-required queue.
A campaign

The Δelo Derby

A research campaign that races training recipes against each other — each idea changes exactly one lever versus a shared control, for clean attribution. The scheduler is a current-elo priority queue: feed the leader. The strongest not-yet-finished idea gets the next chunk; everyone reaches the budget eventually, leaders first.

v1 finding: exploration beats compute for the ceiling. Random openings (open-div4, 1385) and high temperature (temp-16, 1340 peak) topped the compute levers (more sims 1265, more SGD 1284). Compute levers grok faster but peak lower and overtrain; open-div4 ended right at its peak.

v1 → v2: from single-process to production wall-slice

The v1 derby raced single-process (one gomoku.train, GPU only ~30% busy), so its wall-clock numbers were unrepresentative — Jason caught it, and the v1 results were ranked by elo only. The fix in v2: race the production multiprocess recipe (the 1-trainer/8-worker wave-mode bundle, GPU saturated) in wall-slices, so Δelo/hr is finally honest. Each chunk is one run_sweep --max-wall-secs 600 --final-eval slice — Piece 3, used as a campaign primitive.

Operation

How to use it

Start a training slice (a GPU-required item)

python scripts/run_sweep.py --cell WL5 \
    --resume sweep_runs/WL5/checkpoints/latest.pt \
    --max-wall-secs 600 --final-eval

Run a perf cell (pure self-play, the R-S* family)

python scripts/canonical_sweep.py \
    --out-dir sweep_logs/lab-<LANE>-$TS --cells-from cells.csv \
    --lane <LANE> --secs-per-cell 60 [--fp16-eval]

Race the Δelo Derby (a campaign on the queue)

                                    # roster from derby_v2_board.json

Explore = get in the GPU-required queue

A new idea is a lane in wiki/ops/gpu-queue.md: tier it (1 architectural > 2 compound > 3 speculative), give it priority = (E[Δ] × P[succeeds]) / wall_cost, smoke it at 60–90s, file a receipt, and let the Reviewer audit. Code changes for the lane fan out to the parallel queue as Agents in worktrees and integrate with git merge --no-ff (never rebase).

Roadmap

What's next

  • Δelo Derby v2 (queued, next up). The top-3 head-to-head on the production multiprocess recipe in wall-slices, ranked by an honest Δelo/hr. Built but not yet launched — the three derby-* cells are already wired into run_sweep.CELLS.
  • Fix the broken training-cost metric (LF1-followups, Tier-1 top). The cold-window R-TRAIN-* metric measured a pre-buffer-fill transient and called a divergent recipe "+152%". Work: a warm-buffer metric, map the runaway stability knee (bounded ≤ V=384, divergent at V=512), and a per-version tile cap as the architectural fix.
  • Curator / curriculum board (future campaign). Decouple retention (archive everything) from sampling (a curated in-RAM slice). First result is in: recency-weighted ≫ LRU (~63-elo gap). Needs a headroom parent so curators compete on upside, not just forgetting-resistance.
  • ANE residency. Static-batch Core ML export restores genuine ANE residency (first in the lab) but offers no throughput win and no contention immunity at this model size; kept as an opt-in capability for GPU-idle use.
  • Containerize training runs. One container at a time, to lower startup friction. Open question: no Metal/MPS in Docker on macOS, so this targets off-Mac / at-scale or a non-Docker run unit.
  • Buffer scaling. WL5's 1.5M buffer cycled ~28× by 1M games; default to 3M for the next cell, paired with curation as a chip-load lever.
North-star

Δelo / Δt — the elo-gain rate

The metric that ranks everything: Δelo per unit wall-clock — a slope, like delta-v. Measured from a common checkpoint over a fixed window against a stable anchor. Throughput proxies like aug/s and epochs/s are gameable means, not the goal — LF1 proved it, posting "+152% aug/s" on a recipe that diverged in a real run.

Maximize the slope, not the proxy. A recipe can post a sky-high, flat aug/s while real strength stalls or diverges. The north-star is the rate at which anchored elo actually rises against the wall clock — measured, from a common start, versus a stable anchor.

In practice this is operationalized as a metric family: MTTE (minutes-to-target-elo, the primary, runaway-proof by construction) and EPWH (elo-per-wall-hour, the secondary diagnostic slope), both guarded by an inside-noise CI check.
